Start here: distance, speed and time

Every activity in this lesson rests on one simple relationship. Spend a few minutes here first: the units and the tidy notation will save you from most of the mistakes people make later.

1. One relationship, three ways to write it

v = d ÷ t

Speed is the distance covered divided by the time taken.

d = v × t

Distance is speed multiplied by time.

t = d ÷ v

Time is distance divided by speed.

  • Memory triangle. Cover the quantity you want. What is left shows the formula: side by side means multiply, one above the other means divide.
  • Convert first. Put everything in meters and seconds before you calculate. Then the answer comes out in meters, seconds or m/s automatically.
  • Echoes go there and back. When you time an echo, the sound has covered the distance twice, so the distance to the target is d = v t ÷ 2.
  • Sanity check. Ask whether the answer is sensible: a bat’s moth is a few meters away, an organ is a few centimeters deep.

2. Worked examples

Example A: a walk to class

You walk at 1.5 m/s for 2 minutes. How far do you go?

t = 2 min = 120 s
d = v × t = 1.5 m/s × 120 s = 180 m

Minutes were converted to seconds first.

Example B: thunder

You hear thunder 3.0 s after the flash. Sound travels at 343 m/s in air. How far away was the lightning?

d = v × t = 343 m/s × 3.0 s = 1029 m ≈ 1.0 km

The light arrives almost instantly, so the 3.0 s is the sound’s one-way trip.

Example C: an echo from a cliff

You clap and hear the echo from a cliff 0.50 s later. How far away is the cliff?

Sound travels there and back: 343 m/s × 0.50 s = 171.5 m
d = 171.5 m ÷ 2 = 86 m

This is exactly what a bat does in Part 1.

Example D: an echo inside the body

An ultrasound echo returns 65 µs after the pulse. Sound travels at 1540 m/s in soft tissue. How deep is the boundary?

t = 65 µs = 65 × 10⁻⁶ s
d = v t ÷ 2 = 1540 m/s × 65 × 10⁻⁶ s ÷ 2 = 0.050 m = 5.0 cm

Microseconds were converted to seconds first. This is Part 2.

Going to a smaller unit, multiply. Going to a bigger unit, divide.

  • 4.2 cm to millimeters: 4.2 × 10 = 42 mm
  • 350 mm to meters: 350 ÷ 1000 = 0.35 m
  • 12 ms to seconds: 12 ÷ 1000 = 0.012 s = 1.2 × 10⁻² s
  • 0.25 ms to microseconds: 0.25 × 1000 = 250 µs

4. Scientific notation keeps things tidy

Ultrasound mixes very large and very small numbers. Writing them as a × 10ⁿ, with a between 1 and 10, makes them easy to read and easy to calculate with.

Multiplying: multiply the front numbers, add the exponents.
(2 × 10³) × (3 × 10⁻⁵) = 6 × 10⁻²

Dividing: divide the front numbers, subtract the exponents.
(8 × 10⁻²) ÷ (2 × 10³) = 4 × 10⁻⁵

Example D again, the tidy way

d = v t ÷ 2
= (1.54 × 10³ m/s) × (6.5 × 10⁻⁵ s) ÷ 2
= (1.54 × 6.5) × 103 + (−5) m ÷ 2
= 10.0 × 10⁻² m ÷ 2
= 5.0 × 10⁻² m = 5.0 cm

No long strings of zeros, and the exponent tells you the size at a glance: 10⁻² m is centimeters.

d = v t ÷ 2

The call travels out to the target and the echo travels back, so the sound covers twice the distance in the measured time.

vair = 343 m/s, vwater ≈ 1500 m/s

Speeds at about 20 °C. Remember to convert milliseconds to seconds: 1 ms = 0.001 s. In air, 1 m of distance takes about 5.8 ms round trip.

λ = v ÷ f

The physics behind it

d = v t ÷ 2

The pulse travels down to the boundary and the echo travels back up, so the sound covers twice the depth in the measured time t.

v = 1540 m/s = 0.154 cm/µs

The average speed of sound in soft tissue. Every scanner assumes this speed when it converts echo time into depth. Handy conversion: 1 cm of depth takes about 13 µs round trip.

Layer thickness = vlayer × Δt ÷ 2

When layers have different speeds, work down one layer at a time using the time spent in each layer, then add the thicknesses.

Why it matters clinically

Depth measurements guide biopsy needles, size organs and tumors, and track fetal growth. The scanner never measures depth directly: it measures time and assumes a speed.

When sound travels through tissue slower than 1540 m/s, such as a thick layer of fat, echoes arrive late and the scanner draws structures deeper than they are. Faster tissue does the opposite. This is called a speed propagation error, and it can shift or bend structures on the image.

The physics behind it

Z = ρ v

Acoustic impedance: how hard a material is to set vibrating. Units: Rayl, or kg/(m²·s).

R = ((Z₂ − Z₁) ÷ (Z₂ + Z₁))², T = 1 − R

Fraction of intensity reflected (R) and transmitted (T) at a boundary, for sound hitting it straight on.

Round trip = T × T

An echo from inside the body crosses the skin boundary twice, once going in and once coming out.

Loss in dB = 10 log₁₀(fraction)

A fraction of one millionth is −60 dB.

So why gel, and not just water?

Acoustically, any liquid with an impedance near tissue’s would do: water and oil both let almost all the sound through. Gel wins for practical reasons. It is thick enough to stay where it is put and fill every gap on curved or hairy skin, it doesn’t run off or evaporate during an exam, it is water-based and gentle on skin, and it won’t damage the probe’s lens the way oils and lotions can.

The same idea is built into the probe itself. The piezoelectric crystal’s impedance (about 30 MRayl) is far above tissue’s, so a matching layer with an in-between impedance sits on the probe face. The ideal value is the geometric mean of the two: Z = √(Z₁ Z₂).

Values are simplified for teaching and assume sound hits each boundary straight on. The scene treats the coupling layer as one boundary with the skin; a real air gap is even worse, because sound must also leave the probe into the air.
